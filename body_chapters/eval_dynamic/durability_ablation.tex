\section{Pricing Durability: A Configuration Ablation}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability}

\autoref{sec:eval:dynamic:dirty} showed that the configuration used throughout this chapter \emph{defers} the cost of persistence rather than avoiding it.
We disable the write-ahead log and periodic checkpointing so that \project{} behaves as much like an in-memory system as its architecture permits.
This section measures what those mechanisms cost, by ablation: starting from \project{} configured with both persistence mechanisms enabled, we remove one at a time.

No comparison reported in this chapter uses an intermediate configuration; \textsc{none} is the fastest.
The ablation eliminates a hypothesis: if \project{}'s persistence machinery were expensive, the throughput gap against Teseo, LiveGraph, and GraphOne (none of which are run durably either) would need no further explanation.
The measurements below show that the machinery is cheap, which forces the explanation elsewhere.

\subsection{The configurations}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability:configs}

\wt{} exposes two independent persistence mechanisms, a write-ahead log and
periodic checkpointing; each is a binary decision producing the four configurations in \autoref{tab:durability-configs}, ordered by the strength of the guarantee each provides.

The log alone provides a guarantee worth noting.
\project{} commits with \wt{}'s \texttt{sync=off}, which does not disable syncing; it weakens the \emph{commit contract}.
The redo record is buffered in memory and the commit returns immediately, but the record is still forced to disk when a log file fills and rotates and again at every checkpoint.
A crash loses a \emph{bounded} suffix of recent commits rather than the whole run.
% Describing this configuration as offering ``no durability'' is wrong; describing it as offering the durability of a synchronous commit is equally wrong.
This is analogous to the asynchronous commit protocol commonly used by databases such as PostgreSQL~\cite{asyncCommit}; it is also known as delayed durability~\cite{mssqlDelayedDurability} or relaxed durability.

\textsc{wal} is ranked above \textsc{ckpt} because its worst-case loss window is
narrower, not because a log is inherently a stronger mechanism than a
checkpoint. Both give sawtooth protection: a crash immediately after a log
rotation, or immediately after a checkpoint completes, loses almost nothing, and
the exposure then grows until the next one. What separates them is the width of
the tooth. Log files rotate when they fill, which at the $100$\,MB default and
the ${\sim}133$\,MB/s of log this ingest generates is roughly every $0.8$\,s,
whereas checkpoints complete every $78$\,s early in the run, stretching to
$479$\,s near its end. \textsc{wal}'s window is both narrower and stable;
\textsc{ckpt}'s widens as the graph grows.

The two are not points on a single axis, and the ordering should not be read as
one. The log bounds how much committed work a crash destroys; the checkpoint
bounds how long recovery takes. \textsc{wal} with no checkpoint ever taken must
replay its log from the beginning of the run, so its recovery time grows without
bound, while \textsc{ckpt} recovers by opening the last checkpoint and replays
nothing. \textsc{wal-ckpt} heads the list because it is the only configuration
that bounds both.

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability:method}

All runs insert the 260\,M edges of \texttt{graph500-24} into an initially empty graph using the split edge-key layout with 72 writer threads, on machine~A (\autoref{sec:eval:dynamic:setup}).
The \wt{} cache is sized at 800\,GiB so that the graph is resident throughout, producing no eviction pressure; memory-mapped I/O and statistics collection are both off, as they perturb the ingest path.
We report medians.
Only the persistence configuration varies between runs; we hold every other parameter, and the binary itself, fixed.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability:results}

\autoref{tab:durability-ablation} presents the results. The two mechanisms are independent, so the four configurations form a $2\times2$ grid, and each mechanism can be priced with the other held fixed.

The redo log costs $37\%$ of ingest throughput, and costs the same whether checkpointing is on ($-36.8\%$) or off ($-37.0\%$).
Checkpointing costs about $5\%$, again regardless of the log ($-4.9\%$ with it, $-5.2\%$ without).
The two do not interact: the product of the marginal costs predicts the corner where both are enabled to within $0.2$ percentage points of the measured $-40.1\%$.
\project{}'s full persistence machinery therefore costs $1.67\times$ on ingest throughput, taking the system from $1.700$ to $1.019$\,M edges/s, and essentially all of that is the log.

\paragraph{The redo log is the expensive mechanism; checkpointing is not.}
Almost the whole of the $1.67\times$ is attributable to the log.
Removing it lifts throughput by $58\%$ with checkpointing left on and by $59\%$ with it off.
Removing checkpointing is worth about $5\%$ either way, at the edge of what these measurements resolve; we do not claim the two members of that pair are distinguishable, a consequence of \project{}'s update granularity.
Each inserted edge is its own \wt{} transaction, so the log receives one record per edge and its cost scales with the edge count, whereas a checkpoint's cost scales with the volume of dirty pages it must reconcile and is amortised over everything that accumulated since the last one.

\paragraph{Checkpointing is cheap at this scale, and only at this scale.}
The near-equality of \textsc{ckpt} and \textsc{none} should not be interpreted as a global property.
\wt{}'s \texttt{checkpoint=(\allowbreak wait=$n$)} specifies the interval \emph{between} the end of one checkpoint and the start of the next, not a period; as the graph grows, each checkpoint takes longer, so the duty cycle degrades over the life of a run.
In these ingests the observed interval stretches from roughly $78$\,s early in the run to $479$\,s near its end.
A \texttt{graph500-24} ingest completes before that divergence has run its course, so the figures above understate the steady-state cost of checkpointing on a longer or larger ingest.

\subsection{On synchronous commits}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability:sync}

\wt{} can additionally be asked to make each commit durable before it returns, rather than buffering the redo record.
We measured this configuration and do not report it as a rung of the ablation, because at \project{}'s transaction granularity it does not measure what the ladder is measuring.
\project{} commits one edge per transaction, so a synchronous commit forces a durability wait per edge ($260$\,M of them for the ingest above), and the resulting slowdown of roughly $40\times$ is dominated by the per-edge granularity rather than by durability as such.
A system intending to ingest durably would batch many edges into each transaction and amortise the wait across the batch; that is a different experiment, and one we have not run.
We note the measurement here so that its absence from \autoref{tab:durability-ablation} is not mistaken for an oversight.

\subsection{Residual cost}
\label{sec:eval:dynamic:durability:residual}

\textsc{none} removes every persistence mechanism: no redo log, no checkpointing, a cache large enough that the graph never approaches an eviction
threshold.
Removing them was worth $1.67\times$ in total.
Yet \textsc{none} is the configuration behind every \project{} number reported elsewhere in this chapter, including those where the gap relative to the in-memory comparison systems is widest. The ablation therefore excludes the write-ahead log and checkpointing as explanations for that gap.

The remaining cost cannot be eliminated. Disabling the log and the checkpoint server stops \wt{} from \emph{writing}, but leaves the representation untouched: the graph is still held as page-oriented, block-aligned B-tree images with byte-comparable keys, still reconciled when pages are released, still governed by an extent allocator and an always-on eviction protocol.
\project{} pays for remaining \emph{persistable}, and \autoref{sec:eval:dynamic:dirty} shows the same thing from the other direction: the deferred work does not disappear but accumulates.
That cost is a property of the storage format, and no configuration removes it.
